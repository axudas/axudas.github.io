Smart Ring Modulator

A guitar effects pedal that multiplies your signal by a carrier wave. A classic transformer-and-diode ring does the multiplying, and an ESP32 generates the carrier, adds a digital mode, and runs a small display and knob.

Hardware

The PCB

  • 100 × 75 mm board, designed and routed in LibrePCB.
  • Through-hole parts, so anyone can solder it by hand.
  • 0.3 mm signal traces, 0.5 mm power traces, and a ground plane on the bottom layer.
  • ESP32 DevKit (ESP-WROOM-32) as the brain, powered only from its USB-C port. The analog parts run off its 3.3 V rail through an RC filter.
  • 3D-printed enclosure modeled in FreeCAD, with a base and a lid cut out for the jacks, knob, encoder, and screen.
Theory

The math

A ring modulator multiplies the input by a carrier. Multiplying two sine waves makes two new frequencies, the difference and the sum, and neither original frequency survives:

sin(A) · sin(B) = ½ [ cos(A − B) − cos(A + B) ]

This pedal uses a square-wave carrier. A square wave is a sum of odd harmonics whose strength falls off as 1/n:

c(t) = 4/π · [ sin(ωct) + ⅓ sin(3ωct) + ⅕ sin(5ωct) + … ]

So every harmonic of the carrier gets its own pair of sidebands. With the input at f and the carrier at 10f, the output lands at n·10f ± f for n = 1, 3, 5, …, and nothing is left at f or 10f:

Worked example: play an A4 (440 Hz) with the carrier at 10× (4.4 kHz) and the output contains:

3.96 & 4.84 kHz10f − f and 10f + f (strongest)
12.76 & 13.64 kHz30f ± f, one third as strong
21.56 & 22.44 kHz50f ± f, one fifth as strong

None of these are harmonics of 440 Hz, and that's where the metallic, bell-like ring modulator sound comes from.

Circuit

How the diode ring multiplies

The four diodes (D1–D4) sit between two center-tapped audio transformers (T1, T2). The carrier drives T1's center tap, so on each half cycle one pair of diodes turns on and the other turns off. That reverses which way the guitar signal passes through, flipping its polarity every half cycle.

Flipping polarity on a schedule is the same as multiplying by a square wave of ±1. That's why the square-carrier math above describes this circuit exactly. The balanced, center-tapped transformers also cancel the carrier itself, so only the sidebands reach the output.

Design

Signal chain

Input buffer

MCP6002 rail-to-rail op-amp with a 1 MΩ input, biased at 1.65 V so a guitar sees a proper high-impedance load at 3.3 V. It feeds both the diode ring and the ESP32's ADC.

Diode ring

Two ZH23 EI14 600:600 center-tapped transformers (T1, T2) around four 1N4007 diodes. This is the analog multiplier.

Carrier

The ESP32 makes a square wave on GPIO25 (DAC1), sent through 220 Ω and a coupling cap into T1's center tap. A panel pot on GPIO35 sets the frequency.

Digital mode

The buffered guitar goes into the ADC on GPIO34. Processed audio comes out of DAC2 on GPIO26 through a two-stage RC low-pass filter.

Output

Slide switch S1 picks the ring modulator or the digital path, then a coupling cap feeds the output jack.

Controls

A 0.96″ I²C OLED (GPIO23/GPIO22) and a KY-040 rotary encoder (GPIO17/16/4) for menus and settings.
